## 1. Wellbeing and belonging

Your wellbeing comes first. A research degree is demanding, and doing it well depends on looking after yourself, feeling you belong, and knowing support is there when you need it.

### 1.1 Life beyond academia

A pervasive problem in academia is tying one's self-identity and purpose to work and to "being an academic". Given the narrowing scope of opportunities in the field and ever-increasing demands on academic work, this is a recipe for poor mental health. One study of PhD students found that about one in three were at risk of a common psychiatric disorder, and job demands, balancing work and family, and career prospects outside academia were among the factors linked to it ([Levecque et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.respol.2017.02.008)). As a result, we encourage open discussion of your life outside academia and your opportunities beyond it. We also encourage you to make non-academic friends; in our experience, that helps put work hurdles in perspective.

Rest is part of the work. We don't expect you to work evenings or weekends, and messages from us outside working hours never need a reply then. Take your annual leave, switch off properly when you do, and don't feel you need to justify time away. Steady weeks get more done than long nights.

### 1.2 You're not alone

Research can feel lonely. While we aim to create a collaborative, dynamic research environment, academic work is at times solitary, and that can add pressure and mental load that becomes unsustainable. We have been through this and recognise it.

Imposter syndrome is just as common. Seeing seemingly high-flying, overachieving academics around you is challenging, and you may want to project an aura of confidence, as if you know everything, to keep up. **Don't.** **No question is too dumb,** and it is never too late to ask fundamental questions. We will NEVER judge you for asking a question. We have all experienced imposter syndrome too (and still do to this day!).

You don't have to rely on us alone, either. Get to know the other students in the group: share drafts, swap code tips, have lunch together. Students a year or two ahead of you are often the best guide to what's normal. Beyond the group, the [Graduate Student Association](https://gsa.unimelb.edu.au) runs events and programs for graduate students across the University.

### 1.3 Inclusion and belonging

Academia has long been a closed, private club of (mostly) straight white men. Even today, many people coming up in the field don't see role models like themselves ahead of them. It is our personal mission to dismantle this. Inclusion, diversity and equity are at the core of what creates belonging.

We will do our best to support you. Talk to us: communication is key, and we are keen to improve the field. Has someone said or done something that made you uncomfortable, or left you feeling you don't belong? Let's talk it through. If it involves us, or you'd rather raise it elsewhere, the University's [Safer Community Program](https://safercommunity.unimelb.edu.au/) offers support and advice on discrimination, bullying and harassment.

If you have a disability or an ongoing health condition, you don't have to manage it alone or share details you'd rather keep private. The University's [Student Equity and Disability Support](https://services.unimelb.edu.au/student-equity) service can arrange ongoing adjustments to how you study and are assessed. And whatever your circumstances, if you're neurodivergent or simply work best in a particular way, tell us what helps and we'll build it into how we work together.

### 1.4 When things aren't going well

The group culture aims to be as kind and supportive as possible. Every project has difficult stretches, and you are not research machines: you are people with varied lived experiences, concerns and a full life outside your research. We will support you through hard times as much as we can, provided we know about them. Our policy is mutual openness and honesty: we'll tell you when life gets rough for us, and we hope you'll do the same when work gets hard to focus on. Raising a problem early is never held against you; the sooner we know, the more options there are.

- **Talk to us first.** Research setbacks, workload, health, or anything else affecting your work: bring it up at a meeting or ask for a separate conversation.
- **If you'd rather not raise it with us,** Masters and Honours students can contact their Course Coordinator; PhD students can contact the chair of their advisory committee or their department's graduate research coordinator.
- **Adjustments and extensions.** Masters and Honours students can apply for [special consideration](https://students.unimelb.edu.au/course-admin/assessment-adjustments/eligibility-and-supporting-documentation). PhD students can arrange leave, part-time study or extensions through the [Graduate Research Hub](https://gradresearch.unimelb.edu.au).
- **Wellbeing.** The University's Counselling and Psychological Services (CAPS) is free and confidential for all students. Looking after yourself comes before any deadline.
- **Urgent help.** If you're in crisis or worried about your safety, at any hour: call 000 in an emergency, or the University's 24/7 [Mental Health Crisis Support Service](https://services.unimelb.edu.au/counsel/emergencies) on 1300 219 459 (or text 0480 079 188). Lifeline is on 13 11 14.

## 2. Good practice

Looking after yourself is the foundation; good habits build on it. Steady, well-organised work keeps deadlines and write-up from turning into a crunch, which is one of the simplest ways to protect your wellbeing day to day.

### 2.1 Good habits

None of these are formal expectations, but we strongly recommend them, especially with write-up in mind.

- **Write as you go.** Keep running notes and draft sections from the start; the final thesis or report is assembled, not written from scratch at the end.
- **Draft as you go.** Figures, methods and results sections can be drafted as soon as the work behind them is done, which saves a crunch at the end.

### 2.2 What a good meeting looks like

1. Before: send a few lines on what you've done, what you'll show, and what you're stuck on, ideally the day before.
2. During: show the concrete work, discuss problems, agree next steps.
3. After: write down the agreed actions and share them with us, so we all start the next meeting from the same list.

### 2.3 Staying in touch between meetings

Between meetings, get in touch however suits the occasion: Slack, email, a Zoom call, or a chat in person. If you're stuck, don't wait for the next meeting. We don't expect replies outside working hours, and if you haven't heard back, a nudge is always welcome.

The group's Slack workspace is **NumericalEarth**: [join with this invitation link](https://join.slack.com/t/numericalearth/shared_invite/zt-4envhdrqs-N6aa6HxGBJ~4i5Gz99Kzzw).

### 2.4 Giving and receiving feedback

Expect a lot of feedback, especially on your writing. Drafts will come back with many comments and suggested edits: that is normal, and it is how we show you how to develop as a researcher. A heavily marked-up draft doesn't mean you've failed; treat it as a learning opportunity.

How that feedback reaches you is up to you, and we'd like to talk about it early on. Before that conversation, think about:

- **Format.** Do you take in feedback best written, in person, or as a voice recording? Would a quick chat about the overview help before you get detailed written comments?
- **Style.** Do you prefer feedback direct and to the point, or with more context around it?
- **What helps.** Is there anything that makes feedback easier for you to hear and act on?

There is no right answer, and we won't assume you need to change how you take feedback. Every supervisor–student relationship looks different because every student has different learning needs.

Communication goes both ways. Think about how you like to communicate and how you give feedback to others in the group, and tell us what is and isn't working in how we supervise you. You can revisit any of this at any time.
